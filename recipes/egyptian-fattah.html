Egyptian Fattah (Beef over Crisp Pita & Rice with Garlic-Vinegar Tomato Sauce)

DinnerEgyptian150 minServes 6875 kcal41 g protein

Photo: David Berkowitz · CC BY 2.0 · source

The Eid feast dish: toasted pita squares soaked with broth, buttery rice and fall-apart beef, all drenched in a sharp, garlicky vinegar-tomato sauce. Rich, tangy and deeply comforting.

Ingredients

  • 2 lb boneless chuck, in 2-inch chunks
  • 2 cups short-grain or jasmine rice
  • 6 pitas, cut in 1-inch squares
  • 4 tbsp butter or ghee
  • 6 garlic cloves (5 minced)
  • 1 yellow onion, quartered
  • 1 carrot, in chunks
  • 2 celery stalks
  • 2 cups crushed tomatoes

From your pantry: 2 tbsp olive oil, ¼ cup white vinegar, 2 tbsp tomato paste, 1 tsp allspice or 7-spice, ¼ tsp ground cardamom, ¼ tsp sugar, 2 bay leaves, salt, pepper.

Steps

  1. Brown the beef in 1 tbsp butter in a heavy pot over medium-high heat, 6 to 8 minutes. Add the onion, carrot, celery, 1 whole garlic clove, the bay leaves, cardamom, sugar and ½ tsp pepper, cover with boiling water and skim the foam. Simmer partly covered until tender, about 1½ hours, salting near the end. Strain and keep the broth.
  2. Toast the rice in 1 tbsp butter and 1 tbsp oil for 2 minutes, add 2¼ cups of the broth and salt, cover and cook on low 15 minutes.
  3. Bake the pita squares on a sheet at 350°F until crisp and golden, 10 to 12 minutes.
  4. Melt the remaining butter with the last 1 tbsp oil, add the minced garlic and cook until fragrant and just golden, 1 minute. Add the vinegar and simmer 3 minutes. Spoon out half for the topping.
  5. Add the crushed tomatoes, tomato paste, allspice and 1 cup broth to the pan and simmer until slightly thickened, 10 minutes. Season with salt and pepper.
  6. In a deep platter, layer the pita, a ladle of hot broth mixed with the reserved garlic vinegar, then the rice, then the beef. Pour the tomato sauce over the top and serve right away.

Tip: Egyptians often add a piece of mastic resin to the meat broth for its piney aroma. Assemble just before eating so the bread stays partly crisp; Alexandrian cooks skip the tomato sauce entirely.
